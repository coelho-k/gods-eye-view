# Repurposing God's Eye View for Earth's ecosystems

**Yes, with a split verdict.** God's Eye View can become a credible nature-monitoring console with modest engineering. Its small per-layer contract, its two proven ingestion patterns (direct browser fetch, and a cached server proxy) and its existing environmental layers (NASA FIRMS, fire perimeters, weather rasters with a time clock, NASA GIBS imagery) already cover most of what such a console needs. The open-data ecosystem is also rich enough to fill it: NOAA Coral Reef Watch heat stress, forest-disturbance alerts from Global Nature Watch (formerly Global Forest Watch), Hansen annual tree-cover loss, Allen Coral Atlas habitat maps, GBIF and iNaturalist density tiles, and eBird sightings. What the codebase **cannot** deliver, and what no open data source can deliver either, is "highly accurate" monitoring in the everyday sense of the phrase: reef-scale confirmation that corals are bleaching, or the actual live positions of endangered animals. The flagship coral product measures ocean heat, not bleaching, and its skill at warm reefs is near chance. Only about 21% of georeferenced GBIF records state a location uncertainty of 1 km or better. Every responsible wildlife data provider deliberately blurs threatened-species locations, so a live-tracking "God's Eye" for rhinos would be both unavailable and unethical. The realistic product is an **indicator console**: heat-stress, disturbance and fire alerts, protected-area context and observation density, each labelled with its true resolution, latency and confidence. Licensing splits the build in two. A non-commercial or NGO deployment can use nearly everything. A commercial one loses the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA), the IUCN Red List, Global Fishing Watch, the EOX cloudless mosaics and the CC BY-NC share of GBIF and iNaturalist records unless it obtains licences.

> **Naming note.** Two different organisations abbreviate to "GFW". This report writes **Global Nature Watch (GNW)** for the forest platform formerly called Global Forest Watch, and **Global Fishing Watch** in full for the marine vessel platform. Their access rules and licences differ sharply.

## The layer architecture fits; four specific gaps need new work

The codebase was built to put heterogeneous live feeds on a CesiumJS globe, and nature data is just another set of feeds. Every layer is a factory object exposing `init/enable/disable/update/destroy/getStats`, optionally with `getAnalystRecords()` for the voice and analyst system ([CONTRIBUTING.md](../CONTRIBUTING.md); [earthquakes layer](../src/layers/earthquakes/index.js)). All layers are composed in one catalog function ([constructCatalog.js](../src/app/constructCatalog.js)). A central lifecycle manager polls each layer on its `updateInterval`, only while the page is visible, and treats `0` as static or camera-driven ([lifecycle.js](../src/data/lifecycle.js)). A "Coral heat stress", "Protected areas" or "Species observations" layer therefore drops into `src/layers/<family>/` without touching the core.

The two ingestion patterns map cleanly onto nature sources. **Keyless, CORS-open feeds** such as USGS earthquakes are fetched directly by the browser and wired through `createReferenceSources()` ([earthquakes source](../src/layers/earthquakes/source.js); [reference.js](../src/sources/reference.js)). GBIF and iNaturalist tiles, both verified to return `Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *`, fit this pattern ([GBIF MVT probe](https://api.gbif.org/v2/map/occurrence/density/0/0/0.mvt?srs=EPSG:3857&taxonKey=212); [iNat grid probe](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/grid/0/0/0.png)). **Keyed or quota-bound feeds** go through a Vite middleware proxy. The FIRMS proxy is the canonical template: it keeps the key server-side, holds a 30-minute TTL cache in memory and on disk, makes only one upstream request at a time per refresh, serves stale data when the upstream fails, and returns `503 no_key` without ever contacting the upstream when no key is set ([firms.js](../server/providers/firms.js)). The Global Nature Watch key, the eBird key, the IUCN token, Movebank credentials and the Global Fishing Watch bearer token all belong behind copies of it. Global Fishing Watch explicitly forbids putting its tokens in "publicly accessible web interfaces" ([Global Fishing Watch license & rate limits](https://globalfishingwatch.org/our-apis/documentation/docs/license-rate-limits)).

The cost of a new layer is known precisely. The most recent full layer addition, air quality (commit `8887ca47`), touched **26 files and added about 1,730 lines**. The touched files cover the source, model and layer factory; the catalog entry; a share-link token; the credit, `DATA_SOURCES.md` and changelog entries; the panel group; and a headless QA gate. Voice-manifest and i18n entries followed in later commits ([layerManifest.js](../src/voice/layerManifest.js); [layerNames.js](../src/ui/layerNames.js)). Most of that is registration boilerplate. The payoff is that each nature layer immediately becomes shareable by link, queryable by voice, and exposable to Claude/Codex as an MCP tool through `defineTool` ([hazards.js](../src/tools/queries/hazards.js); [TOOLS.md](../docs/TOOLS.md)).

Four gaps need genuine new work, and they decide how big the project is. The first is that **the app has no WMS or WMTS imagery provider**. Nothing in `src/` uses `WebMapServiceImageryProvider` or `WebMapTileServiceImageryProvider`. Rasters arrive only as proxied WMS GetMap frames that the client tiles itself (weather) or as XYZ URL templates (NASA GIBS in Recent Imagery) ([imagery.js](../src/maps/imagery.js); [weather rendering](../src/layers/weather/rendering.js); [recentImagery model](../src/layers/recentImagery/model.js)). The Allen Coral Atlas GeoServer and the Copernicus Marine WMTS both need one of these providers.

The second gap is that **the time clock is hard-wired to weather**. It supports scrubbing and playback, but its products are a frozen enum on both server and client, unknown products throw an error, and selected history times are not carried in share links ([weather source](../src/layers/weather/source.js); [CURRENT-STATE.md](../docs/CURRENT-STATE.md)). Replaying Hansen loss years 2001–2024 or a decade of degree heating weeks needs a generic time-raster layer family. The newly merged per-product `display` metadata (legend, units, extent, credit) is the right seam to build it from ([display.js](../src/layers/weather/display.js)).

The third gap is that **Entity-based polygons will not scale to global protected areas**. User file imports are capped at 20,000 entities and 250,000 positions ([geoFileImport.js](../src/data/geoFileImport.js)), and submarine cables alone, at 2,629 objects, already reach 412 MiB of heap ([PERFORMANCE.md](../docs/PERFORMANCE.md)). Protected-area boundaries need vector tiles (the repo already decodes MVT in [vectorTiles.js](../src/sources/vectorTiles.js)), raster tiles, or viewport-bounded queries like the fire-perimeter ArcGIS paging ([firePerimeters.js](../server/providers/firePerimeters.js)).

The fourth gap is that **Vite middleware is not a production host**. The security docs describe the app as "built for exploration, demos, and learning — not as a hardened production service", and Docker runs Vite exactly as in development ([SECURITY.md](../SECURITY.md); [docker-start.mjs](../scripts/docker-start.mjs)). A public nature site needs the Connect-style providers ported to Express or Fastify, with shared server-side caching. Quotas such as Global Fishing Watch's 50,000 requests a day per user, and iNaturalist's recommended ~10,000 requests a day, all land on the server that makes the calls, not on individual viewers ([Global Fishing Watch limits](https://globalfishingwatch.org/our-apis/documentation/docs/license-rate-limits); [pyinaturalist constants](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pyinat/pyinaturalist/main/pyinaturalist/constants.py)).

Point density is not a gap, because the template already exists. The FIRMS layer handled **100,430 detections** by aggregating them into grid cells (at most 1,800–3,600 cells) when zoomed out and switching to at most 2,500–3,000 individual sprites close in ([firms policy](../src/layers/firms/policy.js); [PERFORMANCE.md](../docs/PERFORMANCE.md)). That is exactly the pattern species occurrences need.

## Marine monitoring measures ocean heat, not coral bleaching

**NOAA Coral Reef Watch (CRW)** is the obvious anchor layer, and the easiest to integrate. It is free US-government data at exactly 0.05° (~5 km), covering 1985 to the present and updated daily with about one day of latency. Products include sea-surface temperature (SST), SST anomaly, HotSpot, Degree Heating Weeks (DHW) and the 7-day-maximum Bleaching Alert Area (BAA). CRW itself says the single-day BAA "does not consistently and adequately identify" heat stress ([CRW 5km products](https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/product/5km/index.php)). The full DHW archive is served through the PacIOOS ERDDAP server. Since January 2024 the CoastWatch ERDDAP keeps only the latest three years ([PacIOOS dhw_5km](https://pae-paha.pacioos.hawaii.edu/erddap/griddap/dhw_5km.html); [CoastWatch NOAA_DHW](https://coastwatch.pfeg.noaa.gov/erddap/info/NOAA_DHW/index.html)). CRW lists no WMS endpoint of its own. The integration is therefore a weather-style proxy: it requests ERDDAP PNG subsets for a date and drapes them, with legend and units supplied through the `display` metadata. In addition, 219 Regional Virtual Stations publish small text time series that suit click-to-chart popups, and they were still updating in late October 2026 ([CRW virtual stations](https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/product/vs/data.php)). The alert scale changed on 15 December 2023 to add Levels 3–5, up to more than 20 DHW for "near complete mortality". Virtual-station alerts and the 4-Month Outlook page still use the old scale, which stops at Level 2, so the UI must reconcile the two ([EcoWatch](https://www.ecowatch.com/noaa-coral-reef-watch-new-alerts-climate-change.html); [CRW Outlook](https://coralreefwatch.noaa.gov/satellite/bleachingoutlook_cfs/outlook_cfs.php)).

"Accurate" needs careful framing for this layer. DHW is an accurate gridded measure of accumulated anomalous heat, but a weak predictor of whether a particular reef actually bleached. Across 100 reef sites from 1982 to 2016, standard DHW reached a maximum Equitable Threat Score of only **0.150**, roughly the skill of a week-ahead rain forecast, although that study computed DHW from OISST rather than CRW's own product ([DeCarlo 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7337031/)). Skill collapses at warm reefs. A July 2026 analysis of 7,286 bleaching records found that DHW's AUC falls to **0.568** where climatological SST is at least 29 °C and to **0.513, effectively chance**, above 30 °C, with the DHW ≥4 alert reaching a sensitivity of just 0.055 in the ≥29 °C band. Warm reefs are the majority case: **63.4% of reef cells** have a maximum monthly mean of at least 29 °C. In Japan, **24 of 26 severe bleaching observations occurred below 4 DHW** ([Fukui 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2026.1835611/full)). Studies seen only in abstract or snippet form report large skill gains from lower thresholds and shorter accumulation windows, which suggests retuning helps, but those figures were not confirmed here ([Coral Reefs 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00338-024-02512-w); [Frontiers 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2024.1438087/pdf)).

The defensible label is "Heat stress (NOAA CRW, ~5 km, daily, 1-day lag)", never "bleaching". A configurable regional threshold is the obvious product improvement.

The **Allen Coral Atlas** supplies the "where are the reefs" basemap. It maps geomorphic zones and benthic cover at 5 m, with 10 m bathymetry, turbidity and seagrass layers, served through a public GeoServer WMS/WFS. The maps are licensed CC BY 4.0, but the Planet imagery mosaic is CC BY-NC-SA, and republishing the *entire* global habitat dataset needs written consent from ASU ([ACA resources](https://allencoralatlas.org/resources); [ACA methods](https://allencoralatlas.org/methods/)). Accuracy is only moderate. ACA self-reports **60–90% overall accuracy** by region. A large validation study, seen only via secondary citation, reported roughly 69% for geomorphic and 66% for benthic classes ([arXiv citing Lyons 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.16730)). An independent check in New Caledonia found just **20–67%** benthic accuracy ([Living Oceans Foundation](https://livingoceansfoundation.org/publication/evaluation-of-the-allen-coral-atlas-benthic-habitat-map-product-for-new-caledonia-using-representative-habitat-observations-from-a-multi‑species-fishery-assessment)). Coral and algae are merged into one class, benthic mapping stops at about 10 m depth, coverage ends at 30°N–30°S, and the imagery dates from 2018–2020 ([ACA resources](https://allencoralatlas.org/resources)). The atlas is a static context layer, not a condition feed.

The other marine sources fill in context. **Copernicus Marine** offers a keyless, catalog-wide WMTS for global SST and chlorophyll, free with mandatory credit, and its FAQ extends that credit requirement to commercial uses ([CMEMS WMTS](https://help.marine.copernicus.eu/en/articles/6478168-how-to-use-wmts-to-visualize-data); [CMEMS citation FAQ](https://marine.copernicus.eu/faq/cite-cmems-products-cmems-credit/)). **NASA PACE** provides free L3 chlorophyll ([PACE PLUG 2026](https://pace.oceansciences.org/docs/mckibben_2026_01_20-PLUG-Meeting-Winter2026.pdf)), though ocean-colour chlorophyll is unreliable over shallow reefs, where light reflected off the seabed contaminates the signal. **OBIS** has a keyless API with gridded and tile endpoints, but licences are set per dataset, and the AWS GeoParquet export is labelled CC BY-NC as a whole ([AWS registry](https://registry.opendata.aws/obis/); [OBIS forum](https://obis.discourse.group/t/license-on-obis-snapshot-data/97)). **Global Fishing Watch** adds apparent fishing effort and SAR vessel detections near marine protected areas, a strong conservation storyline. It runs on a **72-hour delay**, is **non-commercial only (CC BY-NC 4.0)**, and allows 50,000 requests a day ([data availability](https://globalfishingwatch.org/global-fishing-watch-data-availability/); [license](https://globalfishingwatch.org/our-apis/documentation/docs/license-rate-limits)). The repo's AIS vessel layer could be reframed as "ships near MPAs" to show live context alongside it.

Actual bleaching and mortality observations come only from in-situ survey networks: Reef Check via Aqualink, AGRRA and ReefCloud. These are sparse, lag by weeks to months, and have fragmented or restrictive licences. Reef Check Australia, for example, requires a signed licence and a fee ([Reef Check/Aqualink](https://www.reefcheck.org/latest-reef-check-coral-reef-data-available-on-aqualink/); [Reef Check Australia](https://www.reefcheckaustralia.org/data)).

## Forest alerts are precise only at high confidence, and WDPA is a licence wall

Forest disturbance is where satellite monitoring comes closest to "highly accurate", but only within a narrow envelope. **RADD**, Wageningen's Sentinel-1 radar alert system, is unaffected by cloud. It reached a **user's accuracy of 97.6% ± 4.8** for confirmed alerts of at least 0.2 ha in the Congo Basin. Its producer's accuracy of 95.0% carries a wide ±25.8 interval and drops to 83.5% ± 37.5 once clearings under 0.2 ha are included, and 74% of core pixels were confirmed within 24 days ([Reiche et al. 2021](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/abd0a8)). **Integrated alerts**, which combine GLAD-L, GLAD-S2 and RADD on a 10 m grid, deliberately trade precision for recall. Combining the systems raises producer's accuracy, but each system's false positives add up and lower user's accuracy. Only the "highest" tier, where more than one system agrees, is the high-precision product ([GNW Data API](https://data-api.globalforestwatch.org/dataset/gfw_integrated_alerts); [integrated alerts blog](https://globalforestwatch.org/blog/data-and-tools/integrated-deforestation-alerts)). **DIST-ALERT**, from UMD with NASA OPERA, now extends 30 m weekly alerts to all vegetation globally, including grassland, savanna, wetlands and cropland, but it publishes **no accuracy figures** ([GNW DIST-ALERT](https://globalnaturewatch.org/blog/data-and-tools/global-vegetation-disturbance-deforestation-alerts)).

Alerts flag disturbance, not cause. Fire, windthrow, logging and agricultural conversion look alike. The Global Nature Watch Data API needs an `x-api-key` header bound to an allowlist of Origin domains, so the key has to live in a FIRMS-style proxy ([GNW API key guide](https://globalnaturewatch.org/help/developers/guides/create-and-use-an-api-key/); [OpenEPI guide](https://developer.openepi.io/how-tos/getting-started-using-global-forest-watch-data-api)). Exact tile URL templates, key requirements on the tile CDN, and 2026 licence terms were not verified, and should be checked against the live API before building.

The **Hansen/UMD Global Forest Change** dataset, now at version v1.12, provides annual stand-replacing tree-cover loss at ~30 m for 2001–2024. Its gain layer was never updated past 2012. It is distributed as Earth Engine assets and as Google Cloud Storage GeoTIFF tiles ([Earth Engine catalog](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/UMD_hansen_global_forest_change_2024_v1_12)). It is the natural first use for the time-raster family. Plantation harvests count as loss, and method changes make some cross-year comparisons unreliable.

The supporting land layers each carry a caveat. **ESA WorldCover** has independently validated overall accuracy of **76.7%** for its 2021 v200 release, which makes roughly one pixel in four wrong ([WorldCover validation report](https://pure.iiasa.ac.at/18981/1/WorldCover_PVR_V2.0.pdf)). **Meta/WRI 1 m canopy height** claims a 2.8 m mean absolute error, but an independent lidar comparison found it substantially underestimates tall canopies, and it is a 2018–2020 snapshot that "can't be used to map change" ([GEE community catalog](https://gee-community-catalog.org/projects/meta_trees/); [unibz lidar study](https://bia.unibz.it/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Evaluating-the-reliability-of-the-1-meter/991007384663701241); [Wildlabs review](https://www.wildlabs.net/node/11935)).

Fire is the most mature piece and already ships in the repo. VIIRS 375 m nominal-confidence detections have **commission errors below 1.2%** ([Schroeder et al. 2014](https://directreadout.sci.gsfc.nasa.gov/publications_documents/Schroeder_et_al_2014b_RSE.pdf)), while omission of small, short-lived or canopy-hidden fires remains poorly quantified ([NOAA STAR review](https://www.star.nesdis.noaa.gov/JPSS/documents/AMM/N20/Land_ActiveFires_Iband_Beta_Provisional.pdf)). The existing FIRMS layer needs only reframing, for example "fires inside protected areas".

High-resolution imagery got worse. The **NICFI Planet tropical basemaps ended**: Planet imagery was pulled from Global Nature Watch on 1 April 2025. A revival that began on 26 May 2026 covers only the Amazon basin, only near recent alerts, and runs only until **25 May 2027** ([GNW Planet changes](https://globalnaturewatch.org/blog/data-and-tools/planet-imagery-changes-gfw)). EOX's Sentinel-2 cloudless mosaics are free only for non-commercial use ([EOX viewing terms](https://cloudless.eox.at/products/viewing)). The keyless NASA GIBS template already in Recent Imagery, including Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 (HLS) products, remains the safest imagery path.

**Protected areas are the hardest licensing problem in the whole build.** The WDPA terms bar commercial use without written permission from UNEP-WCMC. They define commercial broadly enough to catch some non-profits, and they bar redistribution and sublicensing. Serving self-generated WDPA vector tiles to the public is therefore a grey area even for non-commercial use ([Protected Planet T&Cs](https://protectedplanet.net/c/terms-and-conditions)). The API has also changed underneath integrators. WDPA merged with the WD-OECM database (other effective area-based conservation measures) on 1 November 2025, API v3 was scheduled for shutdown on 1 May 2026, and commercial users are directed to IBAT ([Protected Planet API](https://api.protectedplanet.net/)). For the United States, **PAD-US 4.1** from USGS is the cleaner choice ([USGS PAD-US](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/gap-analysis-project/science/pad-us-data-download)). Its public-domain status is assumed rather than confirmed. MPAtlas adds assessments of how strongly each marine protected area is actually protected on top of WDPA, but has no documented API ([MCI data use](https://marine-conservation.org/data-use/)).

## Species data are presence-only, mostly imprecise, and ethically constrained

**GBIF** is the backbone. It holds **3.89 billion occurrence records**, and its v2 Maps API serves CORS-open PNG and MVT density tiles, including EPSG:4326 tiles that match Cesium's geographic tiling ([GBIF API probe](https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?limit=1); [GBIF maps README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gbif/maps/master/README.md)). Precision is the catch. Of 3.71 billion georeferenced records, only **782 million (21%)** state a coordinate uncertainty of 1 km or better, and **1.82 billion** carry the COORDINATE_ROUNDED flag ([≤1 km probe](https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?limit=0&hasCoordinate=true&coordinateUncertaintyInMeters=0,1000); [issue facets](https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?limit=0&facet=issue&facet.limit=60)). Licensing splits three ways: 2.74 billion records are CC BY, **676 million (17%) are CC BY-NC**, and 476 million are CC0 ([license facet](https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?limit=0&facet=license)). A useful detail for the ethics design is that GBIF can filter by Red List category directly; `iucnRedListCategory=CR` returns 5.7 million records ([CR probe](https://api.gbif.org/v1/occurrence/search?limit=0&iucnRedListCategory=CR)). The app can therefore flag threatened taxa without redistributing IUCN data.

The other species sources each come with a constraint. **iNaturalist** is keyless and CORS-open, holds ~398 million observations and serves PNG grid tiles, but most observations are CC BY-NC. Threatened taxa are auto-obscured: rhino records return `public_positional_accuracy: 31451` m while the API still supplies a lat/lng ([iNat v2 probe](https://api.inaturalist.org/v2/observations?per_page=1&fields=id,geoprivacy,taxon_geoprivacy,obscured,location,positional_accuracy); [rhino probe](https://api.inaturalist.org/v1/observations?per_page=2&taxon_id=43341&obscured=true&quality_grade=research)). Since 31,451 m is the diagonal of a 0.2° cell, drawing that returned point as a pin would manufacture false precision. **eBird API 2.0** is the most "live" biodiversity feed, returning sightings up to 30 days back within a 50 km radius, with reviewer-validity flags. It needs a key sent in the `x-ebirdapitoken` header ([eBird API docs](https://documenter.getpostman.com/view/664302/S1ENwy59)) and hides about 325 sensitive taxa from all public output except grid-cell maps ([Audubon](https://www.audubon.org/magazine/ebird-now-censoring-sightings-325-risk-species)). **The IUCN Red List** API is now v4 and needs a token ([rredlist NEWS](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ropensci/rredlist/master/NEWS.md)). Its range maps are non-commercial and over-predict where species actually occur, and serving them as globe tiles is likely to count as redistribution ([USGS ScienceBase](https://www.sciencebase.gov/catalog/item/553a7ac3e4b0a658d792c982)). **Movebank** allows only one concurrent request per IP. Its documentation says data that owners make public can be read without credentials, yet an unauthenticated study listing returned HTTP 401 in a November 2026 probe, so plan for credentials and per-study licences ([Movebank API docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/movebank/movebank-api-doc/master/movebank-api.md); [401 probe](https://www.movebank.org/movebank/service/direct-read?entity_type=study); [Movebank terms](https://www.movebank.org/cms/movebank-content/general-movebank-terms-of-use)).

"Highly accurate" holds per record at best, meaning an expert-verified identification with small stated uncertainty. It never holds for "where a species is now". Density layers mostly map observer effort, roads and charismatic taxa, so they should be labelled "observations recorded".

The ethics of a "God's Eye" framing need explicit treatment, because the product's name invites exactly the misuse that conservation data systems are built to prevent. GBIF's best-practice guidance generalizes sensitive taxa to 0.1° (~10 km) or 0.01° (~1 km) for public release, and reserves precise data for written agreements. It explicitly covers tracking data ([Chapman 2020, GBIF](https://docs.gbif.org/sensitive-species-best-practices/master/en/)). Lindenmayer and Scheele documented collectors targeting newly described reptiles within months of their locations being published ([ANU](https://science.anu.edu.au/news-events/news/scientists-urged-keep-rare-species-habitat-secret)). Wildlife Insights blurs camera-trap deployment locations and embargoes data ([WI FAQ](https://www.wildlifeinsights.org/faq)). EarthRanger and SMART hold real-time positions of collared animals and ranger patrols behind organisation credentials, specifically to keep them within anti-poaching teams ([EarthRanger API](https://earthranger-support.helpjuice.com/en_US/earthranger-api)).

The build should therefore hard-code six rules. Never render an obscured or high-uncertainty record as a pin; draw its uncertainty cell instead. Apply at least 0.1° generalization and a delay of days to weeks to every CR/EN/VU taxon, even when the source is laxer. Show density and hex bins rather than individuals. Exclude live tracking of threatened species entirely. Never merge iNaturalist, eBird and GBIF copies in a way that could triangulate a blurred point. Treat EarthRanger and SMART as out of scope for any public deployment.

The analyst and MCP tools need the same guardrails, because an LLM asked "where exactly is the nearest black rhino?" must not be able to answer from raw records.

The build maps almost entirely onto templates that already exist in the repo. The table orders the layers by value delivered per unit of new infrastructure.

Phase 0 is less trivial than it looks. `createMilitaryRegistry()` is constructed unconditionally, and the awareness layer depends on the flights, military, vessels and installations layers ([constructCatalog.js](../src/app/constructCatalog.js)), so removal means untangling those dependencies. Each removed layer also needs edits to the catalog, source contracts, voice manifest, panel and names. Removed layers' share tokens must be **retired, not deleted**. CONTRIBUTING.md says tokens 4–9 are free, but the ledger already assigns `4` (air quality) and `5` (S2 GhostMaps), so the next free token is `6` ([layerStateTokenReservations.json](../src/data/layerStateTokenReservations.json)). The intelligence-style HUD prompts need rewriting. The Cesium version should be pinned, because `package.json` declares `^1.124.0` while 1.138.0 is installed, and some code touches EntityCluster internals ([package.json](../package.json)).

Each phase-1 layer is roughly an air-quality-sized change of about 25 files. Phases 2 and 3 each add one reusable rendering capability (a WMS/WMTS provider, a time-raster family, an MVT polygon layer), and these unlock many later layers cheaply.

Licensing determines which product you can ship:

| Tier | Sources |
|---|---|
| **Commercial use OK with attribution** | NOAA CRW, NASA FIRMS/GIBS/PACE, Copernicus Marine, Allen Coral Atlas maps (CC BY 4.0), ESA WorldCover (licence not verified here), GBIF and iNaturalist records filtered to CC0/CC BY, OBIS records filtered per dataset, Meta canopy height (CC BY 4.0) |
| **Non-commercial only** | Global Fishing Watch (CC BY-NC 4.0), WDPA/WD-OECM and its API, IUCN Red List API and range maps, EOX cloudless 2017+, ACA Planet mosaic, CC BY-NC GBIF/iNat/OBIS records |
| **Permission or agreement required** | Movebank per-study data, OBIS-SEAMAP "permission required" sets, whole-dataset ACA republication, Reef Check Australia, IBAT (the commercial route for WDPA and IUCN data), Planet imagery beyond GNW Horizon |

The repo's own baggage matters for a commercial fork too. The code is MIT, but the bundled TeleGeography cable data (CC BY-NC-SA), the Bhote Koshi imagery and flood path (CC BY-NC), and the runtime S2 GhostMaps layer (CC BY-NC-SA) are non-commercial. The free Cesium ion tier covers personal, non-commercial use only ([LICENSE](../LICENSE); [README.md](../README.md); [dataCredits.js](../src/data/dataCredits.js)). Commercial use requires removing these and licensing Cesium ion and Google tiles. Whatever the tier, every new source gets an entry in [DATA_SOURCES.md](../DATA_SOURCES.md) and a credit in `dataCredits.js`. Global Fishing Watch additionally requires a visible "Powered by Global Fishing Watch" credit, and WDPA requires a link to protectedplanet.net.

## Conclusion

Engineering is not the binding constraint on this repurposing. The binding constraints are epistemic and legal. The codebase's best asset for nature monitoring turns out to be its existing ability to attach typed, queryable records to every layer, which feeds the analyst, voice and MCP tools. That system is exactly where honesty about accuracy should live. If each record carries its native resolution, latency, confidence tier and licence, the voice assistant and MCP tools can answer "is this reef bleaching?" with "heat stress is at Alert Level 2 on a ~5 km pixel; no survey confirms bleaching". That answer is more useful, and more accurate, than any pixel colour. The "God's Eye" framing should be inverted for wildlife. The platform's value is in showing pressures on nature (heat, fire, clearing, fishing effort) at fine resolution, while deliberately showing the animals themselves coarsely.

Two things remain unknown and should be checked before committing to a roadmap. First, the exact 2026 terms, tile endpoints and rate limits for Global Nature Watch, the WDPA v4 API, eBird and IUCN; several of their official pages blocked automated reads during this research. Second, whether any independent validation of DIST-ALERT or the integrated disturbance alerts has been published. Until one appears, only the highest-confidence, multi-system alert tier deserves to be called "high precision" in the interface.
